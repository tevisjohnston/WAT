The traffic is not the problem

Most affiliates send their traffic straight to a sales page. Most of those visitors look, leave, and never come back. Whatever that click cost, in money or in time, bought one look at the offer and nothing more.

Lead-first affiliates keep the lead first. They send the visitor to a simple page that offers something useful in return for an email address. Then a short series of emails helps the new subscriber and recommends the offer, not once but several times. The same click now buys many chances to make the sale.

Chapter 1

How to choose an offer that pays you again each month

Your offer is the product you recommend. Choose it before anything else, because your page, your emails and your traffic all point at it.

One-time or recurring?

A one-time product pays you once for each sale. A recurring product, such as a membership or a software plan, can pay you every month the customer keeps paying. You do the work once, and the commission can come again.

One-time productRecurring product
You are paidOnce for each saleEach month the customer stays
Next month starts withNo sales yetThe customers you sent who stayed

Where to find recurring offers

  • Software people use every day: email services, website builders, design and video tools, hosting.
  • Memberships and communities that add new training or content each month.
  • Affiliate marketplaces such as ClickBank, Digistore24, JVZoo and WarriorPlus. Look for offers with recurring payments, sometimes called rebills.
  • The tools you already pay for. Many have an “Affiliates” or “Partners” link at the bottom of their website.

The eight-point checklist

  • It pays recurring commissions. Read the affiliate terms. Do they last as long as the customer stays, or stop after a set number of months?
  • The commission is worth your time. Look at both the percentage and the price.
  • The credit lasts long enough. The cookie length is how long after a click a sale still counts as yours. Longer is better.
  • It already sells. Look for a sales history, or ask the affiliate manager how well the offer sells.
  • Refunds and cancelling are fair. When a customer gets a refund, the seller takes your commission back. Offers that are hard to cancel bring complaints and refunds.
  • The seller supports affiliates with ready-made emails, banners and a person you can contact.
  • It fits your readers. It solves a problem the people you want to reach really have.
  • You would recommend it to a friend. Your name goes on every email you send about it.

The month-three test

A recurring commission lasts only while the customer stays. So before you promote a monthly offer, ask: would a customer still be glad to pay for this in month three? Look for something new each month, happy customers after the first month, and an easy way to cancel.

Pick one offer

List three offers. Run each one through the checklist and the month-three test. Pick the best one, just one, and get your affiliate link for it.

Chapter 2

The page layout that turns traffic into subscribers

Your page has one job: to turn a visitor into a subscriber. It doesn’t sell the offer. It gives away something useful in return for an email address.

What to give away

The free thing is often called a lead magnet. It can be a short guide like this one, a checklist, a cheat sheet, a template or a short email course. A good one:

  • solves one specific problem,
  • takes minutes to use, not hours,
  • and leads naturally to the offer you recommend.

SEND ME THE GUIDE
  1. A short line that names the free thing: “FREE GUIDE”.
  2. A headline that promises one clear result.
  3. A line or two that says what they get, and how fast.
  4. The form: an email address, plus a first name if you want to greet people by name. The button says what they get, such as “Send Me The Guide”, never “Submit”.
  5. A privacy line: “We respect your privacy. Unsubscribe at any time.”
  6. Three to five bullet points, each one a benefit of what is inside.
  7. A second form at the bottom, if your page is long.

Headline patterns

  • How to [get a result] without [a pain]
  • The [number]-step [plan] that [gets a result]
  • Read this before you [do a common thing]

Promise only what your free thing really delivers. A headline that oversells brings subscribers who feel let down by the first email.

Leave these out

The menu, the sidebar, social icons, links to other offers, and video that plays by itself. Every extra link is a way out. One page, one job.

The thumb test

Many of your visitors will see your page on a phone. Open it on yours and ask: Can I read the headline without zooming in? Can I see the form, or a button that takes me to it, without scrolling far? Can I sign up with one thumb, in under 30 seconds? Fix any “no” before you send traffic.

Build your page

Write your headline and three bullet points. Build the page. Then sign up on it with your own email address, check that your first email arrives and that its link works, and run the thumb test.

Chapter 3

The seven emails that do the selling for you

Most people don’t buy the first time they see an offer. A series of emails gives them time, and gives you more chances. Your email service sends the series by itself: email 1 as soon as someone signs up, then one a day.

Rules for every email

  • Help first. Each email is useful even to a reader who never buys.
  • One link. Give the reader one clear thing to click.
  • Short and plain. Write the way you would write to a friend.
  • Honest. Share only real results that you have permission to share, and say that results vary. Tell readers that you may earn a commission. Never invent a deadline.
  • Wanted. Email only people who asked to hear from you. Your email service adds the unsubscribe link to every email.

Chapter 4

Where to get your first 100 clicks for very little money

You don’t need a big budget to start. You need about 100 visitors to get a first read on your page, and you can get them with time, a little money, or both.

Why 100?

A hundred visitors is enough to see whether your page turns visitors into subscribers at all. It isn’t enough to judge everything, so treat it as a first read, not a final answer.

Free sources cost you time

  • Helpful answers. Find the groups, forums and question sites where your readers ask for help. Answer properly. Link to your page only where the rules allow it. Otherwise, put the link in your profile.
  • Short videos. One tip per video, one video a day. End with “The free guide is linked in my profile.”
  • Your own profiles. Share what you are learning, and put your page’s link in your bio.
  • The people you know. Tell them what you are working on. Some have the same problem, or know someone who does.

Paid sources cost money, and get you numbers faster

  • Solo ads. You pay another list owner to email their subscribers about your page. Quality varies a lot from seller to seller. Read reviews, start with a small order, and judge each seller by how many of the visitors sign up.
  • Small ad tests. One audience, one ad, a small daily budget. Stop any ad whose visitors don’t sign up.

Set a budget you can afford to lose, and stop when it is spent.

Three rules for any source

  • Send traffic to your page, not straight to your affiliate link. Your page is where you keep the lead, and many sites don’t allow bare affiliate links.
  • Follow each community’s rules. Spam gets you banned and hurts your name.
  • Use one source at a time until it is steady. Then add a second.

Chapter 5

How to read your numbers, so you know what to do next

Your numbers tell you where to work. Without them, you guess. Check them once a week, not every hour.

Visitorsreach your page
→
Subscriberssign up
→
Opensread your emails
→
Clicksvisit the offer
→
Salesearn a commission

The four numbers that matter

NumberHow to work it outIf it is low, work on
Sign-up rateSign-ups ÷ visitorsYour page: the headline, what you give away, the thumb test
Open rateOpens ÷ emails deliveredYour subject lines and your “from” name. Check that you aren’t landing in spam.
Click rateClicks ÷ emails deliveredYour emails: more useful, one clear link, a clear reason to click
Earnings per clickCommissions ÷ clicks to the offerThe offer: does it fit your readers, and does its page match what your emails say?

If you pay for traffic, add one more: cost per subscriber, which is what you spent ÷ new subscribers.

Treat the open rate as a rough guide. Some email apps open every email by themselves, so opens can look higher than they are. Clicks are the number to trust.

An example, to show the math

These numbers are made up. They only show how the sums work. Your page gets 200 visitors, and 50 sign up: a sign-up rate of 50 ÷ 200 = 25%. Your 50 subscribers get 7 emails each, so 350 emails are delivered. If 140 are opened, your open rate is 40%. If 21 links are clicked, your click rate is 6%.

Three rules for reading numbers

  • Wait for enough data. Judge a page after about 100 visitors, and a subject line after a few hundred emails.
  • Change one thing at a time, so you know what made the difference.
  • Compare with your own last week, not with someone else’s screenshot.
